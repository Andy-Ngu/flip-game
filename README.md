# Stack Seven

A push-your-luck card game for 3 to 8 players, built with React Native, Expo and TypeScript. Play against 2 to 7 computer players at three skill levels; online tables are next on the roadmap.

On your turn you either **hit** (draw a card) or **stay** (bank your points). Draw a number you already hold and you bust, scoring nothing for the round. Collect seven different numbers and you complete a **Seven Stack** for a bonus and end the round on the spot. The first player to reach the winning score (150, 200 or 250, your choice) wins.

## Features

**The game**
- **Complete rules engine** for a 94-card deck, scoring, special cards, ties and multi-round games, with no React in it so it can be tested by plain scripts
- **Computer opponents** at three levels (Easy, Normal, Hard), checked against each other in a bot tournament
- **Create Table**: 3 to 8 seats, computer skill, winning score (150, 200 or 250) and a turn clock of 15, 30 or 60 seconds
- **Turn clock** on every turn. Run out and you stay automatically; miss three turns in a row and a computer covers your seat until you press "I'm back"
- **Remembered profile**: a drawn character, a colour and a generated name such as "Daring Fox", plus your record (games, wins, win rate, best round, Seven Stacks, top score). "Delete my profile and data" removes it all

**The table**
- **Your hand is always the main stage.** The other players are tiles that show their real cards, their score and their status; the deck shows only how many cards are left
- **Bust odds and a Seven Stack meter** under your hand (the odds can be switched off)
- **Round start board** that lists everyone by score and shows who plays first, then a dealt table with the play order on each tile
- **Choosing a player** for a Time Out, Draw Three or spare Shield dims the table and lights up everyone you can pick
- **Clear moments**: a crown for the leader, a glow on players near the target, a pulse when you are one card from a Seven Stack, a "7 again!" circle on the card that busted you, a Shield moment that shows what was blocked, a calm shuffle notice (with its own riffle sound) when the deck runs out
- **Help when you need it**: three first-game tips, a one-line pop-up the first time each special card appears, press-and-hold any card to read about it, and a picture-based How to Play
- **Quick chat and emotes** (preset only, no typing), which can be hidden

**Feel**
- **Card game feel**: cards fly in face-down and flip, spring into a fanned hand, wobble gently on your turn, lean toward your finger and droop when you bust
- **Score counting**: when you stay, your cards light up one after another with a rising note and a firmer buzz, and the last one gets a bigger finish
- **Sound effects and haptics** tuned to each moment, all generated by a script (no recordings)

**Made for everyone**
- Text size (Normal, Large, Largest), High contrast, Reduce motion and Reduce flashes (the phone's own Reduce motion setting is respected)
- Every control has a label for screen readers, touch targets are 48 px or more, and an Ignore double taps option
- Swap the HIT and STAY sides if your thumb prefers it; game speed from Slower to Faster

## How to play

Each player starts a round with one card. Players then take turns in seat order, starting with the player after the dealer. The dealer changes every round.

**On your turn**, hit or stay.
- **Hit:** draw a card from the deck.
- **Stay:** lock in your current score and sit out the rest of the round. You need at least one card to stay.

**Number cards (0 to 12).** There are as many copies of each number as its value (twelve 12s, eleven 11s, down to one 1, plus a single 0). Draw a number you already have and you **bust**: your hand scores 0 this round.

**Action cards** are resolved when drawn:

| Card | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Time Out** | You choose a player. They bank their points and are out for the rest of the round. |
| **Draw Three** | You choose a player. They must draw the next three cards, and they bust if any is a duplicate. |
| **Shield** | Absorbs one duplicate, then is used up. You can hold one at a time. A spare goes to another player who has none. |

**Bonus cards** change your score: **Bonus +2, +4, +6, +8, +10**, and **Double Score**, which doubles the total of your number cards (not your bonuses).

**Seven Stack.** Collect seven different numbers and the round ends immediately. You get +15, and everyone still in the round banks their points.

**Scoring a hand:** add up the number cards, apply Double Score if you have it, add any Bonus cards, then add 15 for a Seven Stack. A busted hand scores 0.

**Winning.** When a round ends and someone has reached the winning score (150, 200 or 250, chosen when the table is created), the highest total wins. If the top players are tied, everyone plays another round.

## Architecture

The game is split into two layers that don't know about each other's details.

```
src/
  game/          Pure TypeScript. No React, no React Native.
    types.ts, deck.ts, rules.ts, scoring.ts, round.ts   cards, the deck, the rules of one round
    session.ts     A whole game, including pausing for a player's choice
    bot.ts         The computer players
    events.ts      "What just happened?" for haptics, sounds and effects
    sounds.ts      Which sound effects play for each moment
    seats.ts       The table setup: seats, computer skill, winning score, turn clock
    clock.ts       The turn clock's rules (how urgent it feels, when a seat is handed to a computer)
    results.ts     Ranked results and the round-start board
    moments.ts     Leader, near the target, one card from a Seven Stack, deck running low
    odds.ts        The chance that the next card busts you
    countSteps.ts  The score counting after you stay: what lights up, when, and how it sounds
    breakdown.ts   How a hand's points add up (the tooltip on your total)
    tableMetrics.ts  How big everything is drawn so eight players fit on a small phone
    profile.ts, stats.ts, settings.ts   who you are, your record, and what is saved between launches
    chat.ts, cardHelp.ts   preset phrases and emotes; the one-line card explanations
    played.ts      The words of "Gus played Draw Three on you", shown before the card does anything
    check-*.ts     Test suites
  components/    React Native screens and pieces
    ui/            Buttons, panels, dialogs, sheets, text: the shared pieces
    cards/         Card faces and the fanned hand
    avatars/       The drawn characters
    table/         The table screen, the opponent tiles, your stage, the dock, the game hook
    overlays/      Round start board, popups, Shield moment, "played on you" message, Seven Stack celebration
    sheets/        Menu, standings, chat, emotes and the leave dialogs
    results/       Round results and the winner screen
    help/          First-game tips and card help
    screens/       Title, Create Table, Settings, How to Play, Profile
  app/           Expo Router entry point (one screen that switches between the others)
assets/
  source/make-sounds.py   Builds every sound effect (a wooden table theme) from sine waves and noise
  sounds/                 The generated .wav files
scripts/
  run-checks.js  Compiles and runs every test suite (npm test)
```

### Decisions worth explaining

**A pure, immutable game engine.** Every function takes a game state and returns a new one; nothing is changed in place. That makes the rules easy to test without a phone or a screen, and it means the UI can simply hold one piece of state and replace it after each move.

**Pausing for a choice without a state machine.** Some cards need a decision mid-turn (who gets the Time Out?). Instead of splitting each turn into many half-finished states, the session runs the whole move, and if it needs an answer it doesn't have, it stops and remembers the answers so far. After you answer, the move is re-run from the start with one more answer. This is safe because the engine never changes its inputs.

**A bug the tests caught.** Re-running a move means every step must repeat exactly. A stress test showed one step that didn't: when the draw pile ran out mid-turn, the discard pile was reshuffled randomly, so a replay could deal different cards than the player had been asked about. The fix was a seeded shuffle that is stored in the deck state, so the same state always reshuffles the same way. There's now a regression test for it.

**Events instead of haptics in the game logic.** The engine knows nothing about vibration or animation. A function compares the previous and next game state and returns a list of events (bust, Time Out, Seven Stack, and so on), and other functions map those events to a haptic feel, a sound, a toast or a celebration. Changing how the game feels never touches the rules.

**One hook runs the table.** `useTable` holds the game state and everything that happens around it: the computer's moves and their short thinking pause, the turn clock, the deal, sounds and buzzes, the end of a round. The table screen only draws what the hook says and passes taps back.

**A table that fits every phone.** Eight players, the biggest text size and a small screen is a hard layout problem. `tableMetrics` is a pure function that picks the largest of three sizes that fits (and lets the tiles scroll if even the smallest does not), so it is tested like the rules.

**One draw order for everything on the table.** On a phone, "what is in front" is easy to get wrong: a number set on a card in your hand can end up in front of a pop-up, because React Native's new renderer flattens plain views and sorts the whole screen by that number. The fix is a single ladder, in `components/layers.ts`: the table at the back, then the glow, small messages, big moments (Shield blocked, a card played on you, the shuffle), the messages that explain the game, whole scenes (round board, results, winner) and, in front of all, menus. Every layer is a view that is never flattened away, so a number deeper inside (your cards) can only reorder things inside its own layer. The same idea orders the parts of your own section: the bars at the back, your cards, the header, then the BUST stamp. The order follows what design systems say about messages (the more it matters, the nearer the front; one small message at a time; long enough to read; closes when tapped).

**Accessibility is settings, read in one place.** Text size, high contrast, reduce motion, reduce flashes and game speed are turned into one `Ui` object (`AppContext`). Every screen reads that object instead of checking settings itself, so a new screen cannot forget to honour them. All text goes through one `T` component and all taps through one `Tap` component.

**Overlays are views, not modals.** Popups, sheets and dialogs are absolutely positioned views inside the table, so the game can pause and resume around them and the Android back button always closes the top one first.

**Computer players you can trust.** Easy stays at a fixed score, Normal at a higher one, and Hard weighs the expected gain against the chance of busting using the cards it has seen. I didn't want to just claim the levels were different, so the bot suite plays a 3,000-game tournament between all three and checks that Hard wins well above an even share.

**Animation that stays smooth.** Each card's position, tilt, flip and press state live in Reanimated shared values, so animation runs on the UI thread even while JavaScript is busy. The fanned layout is a pure function (`handLayout.ts`) and is tested like the rest of the game logic. The idle wobble only runs for the player whose turn it is, and stops for everyone else.

**Sounds made from nothing.** I didn't want to hunt for sound files with unclear licenses, so a short Python script builds every sound from sine waves and noise, in a "wooden table" theme: notes are a synthesized marimba (a sine wave with two fast-fading overtones and a tiny mallet click), a card is a puff of low-passed noise like a slide over felt, and a bust is a dull thud on wood. I picked the theme by auditioning four candidates (wooden table, music box, casino lounge, soft synth) and choosing the one that stays gentle when it plays a hundred times a game. They are original, the script is in the repo, and changing a sound means changing a number and running it again. Which sound plays is decided by a pure function over the same events the haptics use, so it is tested like the rest of the game.

**Saved settings that can't break the app.** Whatever is read back from the phone is untrusted: it can be missing, cut off, from an older version of the app or edited by hand. `parseSettings` checks every field on its own and falls back to the default, and a saved table must pass the same rules as the Create Table screen, so a bad save can never crash the app or start a game the screen would refuse.

**Cards that look right on every phone.** Card text ignores the phone's font-size setting, and the Time Out and Shield icons are drawn with shapes instead of font symbols, because symbols render differently from one Android font to the next.

## Testing

```
npm test
```

This compiles and runs every suite in `src/game` (20 suites, about 820 checks). The suites cover the rules (rounds, sessions, 300 simulated full games), the computer players (including a 3,000-game tournament), seats and settings, events, sounds, the turn clock, big moments, profile and stats, the table layout, the score counting, the score breakdown, chat, card help and the "played on you" message.

A typical tournament result over 3,000 games (it varies a little each run):

| Level | Win rate |
| --- | --- |
| Easy | about 9% |
| Normal | about 40% |
| Hard | about 51% |

The screens are checked with the TypeScript compiler (`npx tsc --noEmit`) and by playing on a real phone. The automated tests focus on the engine, where a bug is hardest to spot by eye.

### Continuous integration

Every push to the main branch and every pull request runs `.github/workflows/ci.yml` on GitHub Actions, as two independent jobs:

- **Type-check and tests:** `npx tsc --noEmit`, then `npm test`.
- **Build check:** `npx expo export --platform android`, the bundling step Expo does before an app is built, so a missing file or a broken import fails here instead of on a phone.

A new push cancels the run still going for the previous one, and the jobs only get read access to the code.

## Running it

You'll need Node.js and the Expo Go app on your phone (or an Android or iOS emulator).

```
npm install
npx expo install react-native-svg expo-font @expo-google-fonts/lilita-one @expo-google-fonts/nunito
npx expo start
```

Scan the QR code with Expo Go. Haptics need a real phone.

## What I learned

- **TypeScript as a safety net.** My first compiler errors were a property missing from a type and a card kind I hadn't declared. Reading those messages taught me what the types were for: card kinds are a union type, so the compiler stops me from handling a card the game doesn't have.
- **Why you return a new array instead of changing the old one.** The game engine never edits a game in place. Each move returns a new state. That made the rules easy to test, and it's the same habit React state needs.
- **Keeping rules and screens apart.** The whole game runs without React, so I could test it with plain scripts and then build screens on top of it.
- **Testing by simulation.** Playing hundreds of random games, and thousands of bot games, found a rare reshuffle bug that no hand-written test would have thought of.
- **Animation with Reanimated.** Shared values, springs, timing and repeating animations. I studied how Balatro gets its card feel (tilt toward the touch, a shadow that moves, a hand that follows its slot) and rebuilt the techniques with my own artwork.
- **Real devices behave differently from the code.** On my Galaxy S24, the phone's font-size setting broke the card layout. I fixed it by making card text ignore font scaling and drawing the icons as shapes.
- **Designing before coding.** I mocked up every screen first and agreed the details (what appears when the deck runs out, how choosing a player works, what the accessibility settings do) before writing the screens. Changing a mockup is far cheaper than changing code.
- **Working with an AI assistant.** I learned to give specific feedback from the real phone, to ask why a change works before accepting it, and to keep the tests as the judge of whether the code is right.

## About this project

Stack Seven is an independent hobby project made for learning and for my portfolio. It is not affiliated with any publisher, and it uses no artwork, text or assets from another game. It is inspired by the push-your-luck card game genre, and its animation feel is inspired by the way Balatro handles cards.
